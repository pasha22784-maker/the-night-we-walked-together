A Winter Night in Sambhal — The Unspoken Touch

The True Beginning · Winter 2002 · Sambhal

She was looking very beautiful. His eyes were going on her automatically, he was trying very hard not to see her, but he could not help. This was not the first time, he had seen her, but he did not know why it was happening to him this time. She was his maternal sister’s in-laws and he knew her already, but this time, his heart was rebellious to him, beating hard and compelling him to see her. He was only nineteen and she was also just sixteen, maybe it was only infatuation, but he was looking at her, how she smiled, how she talked, everything was new to him. She was talking to him, joking to him.

They were at the Anwar’s would be Bhabhi Jaan’s house. Anwar’s elder brother was recently engaged. They were visiting to his Bhabhi Jaan. In this first meeting she was very frank to him. They knew each other by names but perhaps they never talked or noticed. He had some feeling arousing. Some other people was there, Zakra, Anwar’s sister, Gudiya, her sister, Rukhsar, Bhabhi Jan’s younger sister. He had some strange feeling that time he had never felt before. Though this was not the first time he liked a girl, he had affairs before, but this was not the affair type. He had never felt like this before.

Something pressing lightly, near his left arm, in the chest, which was giving some strange type happiness. If, Shalini also was feeling the same, yes, her name was Shalini. If, she had something in her chest which was beating very fast. No, he thought, she was light in her expression, laughing full of heart, nothing was there like him. It was very beautiful all around. They were coming back to Anwar’s house. It was the winter season, all roads were empty and dark, and some street lights were beaming lightly at frequent distance. It was 11 in the night, fog was all around, their laugh, their talk were echoing. Everyone was talking to each other, cracking joke on each other, but now he was just smiling at the jokes aimlessly as he was busy in his strange thoughts which were feeling sweet to him.

He was looking at her from his eyes’ corner, she was walking next to him and was lost in her laugh with them, suddenly her hand brushed his hand, it was very soft and cold, some current like feelings ran in his body from her touch. He curiously looked at her but nothing was there. She was busy in her chat. Nothing was there he was thinking about, but her touch made him more uncomfortable.

He did not know where he was going, the direction he was supposing to take, was completely blurred. What would be ahead, no one knew, but this time that blurred roads were beautiful. He had no time to think beyond that moment, beyond that blurred road, beyond that charming face.

The Morning Walk & Habeeb's Teasing

Life was never so cheerful before, he could not sleep properly whole night, that strange feeling was pinning him. He was smiling whole night, don’t know why, but something was there. That smiling face was there whole night. Whenever he tried to sleep that face was coming and calling his name again and again. What was that, if she was really calling him? If, she was really thinking about him, If, she was feeling the same way he was feeling. He was asking this question to himself and trying to solve that typical riddle. It was the feeling he could not describe. It was early morning he left the bed and went out the house.

Fog was everywhere, nothing could be seen except few feet distance. But he was on the road, looking through the fog. Something was there he could see through the fog. That smiling face, full of life, full of energy, full of warmness. Few lights were beaming at distance. He never thought that this could be happen with him, he had never imagine that he could feel that way. The construction work was in process at his house, there were five rooms and five shops in the ground floor, facing the main high way road, and some rooms were building at first floor. Only a small tea stall was there in one shop and left were supposed to be let out.

In that cold morning only a few people were on the road, he was walking in the straight line heading to nowhere. It was too cold to go out on the road, but he was walking without any extra cloths to be safe from this chilled breeze which were washing his face and making his nose red at the tip. After a small walk houses were vanished from the sight only vast land which was farmed wheat, and a cemetery at the left side, every one of his family passed away was buried there and he would also be buried there. He had been watching all these things from his childhood but today everything was new to him.

When he was busy in his strange thought and walking with slow paces, someone tapped on his shoulder, he just startled and watch over his shoulder, a familiar face was smiling at him, he could not recognised once, then again he startled as if he was coming from a different place

“What happened, I am calling your name from the Chauraha(intersection) and you are not listening to me, where were you dear?” He said rubbing his hand to make them warm.

“Nothing Yaar(dear), just like that”, he could not replied suddenly “I was just taking a morning walk”. Eeverything disappeared and he felt that he had been thrown down from a very high place.

“I have never seen you taking a morning walk before, what did make you suddenly to do so?” he was asking in funny a way.

“Nothing special, just awakened early today so I thought to take a walk” he was trying to be normal.

“I can’t believe it, there is something you are holding back” he was teasing him now.

“Leave it, and just concentrate on your walk” He made an annoying face.

“Teri Subah Keh Rahi He Teri Raat Ka Fasana(Your morning is describing your night story)” suddenly his heart started beating fast, this line made him more uncomfortable and Habeeb was enjoying the situation now and eyed on him suspiciously.

“What do you mean by this” he was trying to make a normal face while he was feeling strange air filling his stomach and was breathing heavily.

“I know where you were last night” He said proudly putting his hand in his pocket as he was shivering with cold

“You know what!”

“Where you were”

“Where?, by the way”

“At Rukayya Khala’s house”

“Then”

“With whom?”

“Shaaaaal” he did not complete the name

“So what” He said flatly

“Don’t hide it to me, I can help you in this” he made his eyes balls round.

“What” He felt that his heart jumped to his throat.

“Tu Soch le Hum Sochne Ka Mauka Bhi Denge (I am giving you the chance to think and decide)” he was teasing him by singing a Bollywood song.

“Go to hell” He could not answer his question and started running back to home.

“She is in my relation, I can talk to her” Habeeb called in a loud voice behind him, but he did not reply and shook his head in negative without looking back and disappeared into the fog.

The Roots of Sambhal & The Clan

Heritage & Family · 2002 · Sambhal

It was a small town, people living there was almost the same community and knew each other by name and also related to each other from the father’s or mother’s side. The whole community was Turk and supposed to come from Afghanistan in the earlier period of Mughals. His father was from a small family, only two generation was known. His grand- grandfather was supposed to come from Afghanistan in latest time of Mughals. His mother was from a landlord family and having a very big clan, her brother was the Mukhiya (Head) of the town. With the time this tradition had faded out. So, he had almost all the relations from his mother’s side.

Habeeb was his good friend and was related to him from his mother’s side. His father had shifted to Delhi in his twenties and had been living there ever since. He had a big family, four sisters, two of them married to his second Phuppo’s (father’s sister’s) sons, three brothers including him. He was the second last member of his family. The tree of family was, Moin the father & Irfana the mother, Chaman the elder daughter, Gulshan the second daughter, Mumtaz the third daughter, Faheem the elder son and numbered after Mumtaz, Bobby the fourth daughter, Nadeem the second son (himself) and finally Waseem the third son and the youngest in the family. He used to feel that his parents loved him least among their children, but he was the dearest among all his siblings, also in his friends.

He was thinking who told Habeeb about the previous night. It was a normal thing to visit someone’s house, then why Habeeb mentioned it in their talk. He had not told anybody about his feelings then how Habeeb came to know it. He could not understand. He could not name the feelings what was that, and the person he met early morning came to know everything. What it was. He was in confusion. He was not a judgemental type person actually. He used to take time to reach any decision and was famous to solve the problem of others. All his friends used to bring their problems to him, always he used to give the perfect advice but, this time he was confused, what to think, what to do. Unknown feelings were squeezing his heart and he was unable to come over this situation.

Chapter Six

A Marriage House Wakes

Nadeem heard Ammi’s voice from downstairs, floating up through the still morning air. He walked to the left edge of the roof and peeped into the aangan (patio).

Ammi was standing near the chulah (mud stove) in the corner outside the kitchen, elbows planted firmly on her hips — that determined posture she always had when running the house. A faint curl of smoke rose from the chulah, carrying the earthy smell of burning wood and dried cow dung. That side of the house had only one big room, with the kitchen tucked behind it. They rarely cooked inside; most of the meals were prepared out here in the aangan to keep the smoke out of the rooms. On the opposite side were three rooms in an uneven row, and he was on the roof of the last room toward the back.

“Ji, Ammi?” he called.

“Come down, I’m making tea. Bring rusk and biscuits for breakfast,” she said briskly, walking toward the entrance. The hand pump stood beside it, its iron body cold and wet from the night’s mist.

“All are sleeping yet. Why are you making tea so early?” he asked, heading toward the narrow staircase. The damp winter air nipped at his cheeks, and his breath hung like smoke in front of him.

“It’s a marriage house — lots of work to be done. No one can sleep this late. You bring the nashta (snacks), I’ll wake them up,” she replied, gripping the pump handle in one hand and holding the tea pan under the spout with the other. The pump groaned with each push, splashing cold water into the pan in a steady rhythm.

“Take out some money from my purse — it’s in the cupboard in the room,” she added, raising one hand toward the room while still bent over the pump.

He stepped into the room, the air inside warmer, smelling faintly of quilts and sleep. “Wake up, lazy people!” he called out, his voice bouncing off the mud-plastered walls.

It was dark, except for a thin blade of light slipping through the wooden window. The cupboard was fixed into the wall about three feet above the floor, directly opposite the door. A cot sat right under it, piled high with blankets.

He reached for the cupboard, pulled the door open hard — and thud! The lower corner smacked into the head of the person sleeping there, completely wrapped in a blanket.

First came the sound — a sharp tak. Then a loud scream. And finally, a groan.

It was Faheem. Still half-asleep, he clutched his head and muttered in a drowsy, offended tone, “Are you here to take money or my brains?”

From somewhere under the blanket came another muffled line, “If you wanted to wake me, you could’ve just called my name — not attacked me like a dacoit.”

He couldn’t help but laugh as he reached past him into the cupboard.

Faheem pulled the blanket tighter around himself, one eye peeking out just enough to glare at him. Nadeem ignored him and dug into the cupboard, feeling around between neatly folded clothes, a small pile of envelopes, and the soft crinkle of a plastic bag.

The purse was there — dark brown leather, worn soft with age, smelling faintly of attar. As he pulled it out, his fingers brushed against one of the envelopes. Its corner was torn, and he could see neat handwriting inside, the ink faded to a deep brown. The paper felt warm from being tucked away so long.

For a second, he froze. This wasn’t like the bills or receipts Ammi kept; this had weight, a kind of silence about it. His eyes lingered, wondering whose words were trapped inside. But Ammi’s voice from outside broke the spell.

“Hurry up, Nadeem!” The pump handle clanged once more, metal against metal.

He slipped the purse out and closed the cupboard door slowly this time, making sure not to repeat the head-banging incident. Faheem was still muttering under his breath, something about “compensation” and “medical expenses” as he rolled over dramatically.

Stepping into the aangan, he handed Ammi the purse. She set the pan down near the chulah and began counting out a few crumpled notes, her fingers moving quickly despite the morning chill. The fire crackled softly, its glow painting her face in shades of orange.

“Go to Najeeb’s shop,” she said, tucking the notes into his hand. “Get milk, some more sugar, and fresh rusk. The ones in the tin are hard enough to break teeth.”

He smirked. “Should I also get a helmet for Faheem?”

Ammi gave him one of her looks — the kind that made you want to run the errand faster.

The Truck Ride for Wood

Kais came inside and called Faheem. “Jamal has brought the truck. We have to bring wood for the marriage food. He said to ask all the boys in the house.”

At once there was noise in the aangan. Nadeem, his younger brother Waseem, Razi and Shakeel (the younger brothers of Kais), and some boys from the neighbourhood gathered. Faheem also joined, and Jamal’s friends were already outside. They all climbed into the truck standing at the gate.

For the boys it was like flying. The truck moved with a roar and crossed the chauraha, leaving behind the tea stall and the shops. The air hit their faces with force. Their hair and shirts were flying, beating against the wind. It felt cold and fierce, but also gave a thrill, like freedom. Sometimes the road dipped and the whole truck jumped; their hands held the wooden fencing tightly.

On both sides of the narrow road, tall trees leaned over. Their branches reached across, and now and then one would sweep low over the truck. Whenever it came, the three boys quickly lay flat behind the wooden fencing, laughing as the branches brushed over them.

The truck’s engine groaned loudly as it rolled over uneven ground. Each pit and bump shook them, pushing them right, left, forward and back. But none of them minded. For them it was pure fun. They were shouting, laughing, sometimes singing, their voices mixing with the sound of the diesel engine.

The journey took about thirty minutes. Slowly, the road grew lonelier, the fields thicker, and then the jungle appeared, dark and deep, waiting for them with the pile of wood they had to bring.

The truck left the main road and turned onto a kachcha path. Dust swirled in the air, settling on their clothes and hair. On both sides were open fields, scattered bushes, and groups of trees spread here and there. This was the village jungle — not very dense, not very dark — but wide and full of life. Birds chirped from the branches, and a few buffaloes were tied to trees, chewing lazily.

After a short drive, the truck stopped near a clearing where stacks of dry branches and medium-sized logs were arranged. This was the wood meant for cooking — not the heavy tree trunks, but the kind that burned fast and steady under a chulah. Thin branches were tied in bundles, thicker pieces stacked separately. The smell of dry wood and leaves filled the air.

A few men were waiting there with their ox-carts, leaning on sticks, talking lazily until the truck arrived. As Jamal and the elders jumped down from the cabin, greetings were exchanged, then the bargaining began. Prices went up, then down, then up again, with much hand-waving, laughter, and stubborn faces. Finally, a fair deal was agreed upon.

Meanwhile, the younger boys could not sit still. Waseem and Shakeel picked up light sticks and started fencing like swordsmen. Razi tried climbing a low tree, while Nadeem stood laughing, warning him not to fall. Their voices echoed in the open space, mixing with the sound of birds.

The real work began. Men and older boys lifted the bundles of wood and passed them into the truck. The smaller branches made a crackling sound as they were thrown down, filling the empty wooden floor. Lighter pieces were easy for everyone to carry, so even the younger boys tried to help — proudly lifting small bundles and dragging them to the truck, though stopping soon with tired arms.

As the pile of wood grew inside the truck, the space filled with the dry, sharp smell. Bits of leaves and dust stuck to their shirts and hair. Sweat shone on faces, but no one complained. This was marriage work — tiring but joyful. Jamal kept teasing the boys, saying, “Don’t hide, this wood won’t load itself,” and everyone laughed.

After an hour, the truck was full, piled high with dry branches and medium logs neatly arranged. The ox-cart men counted their payment, smiling with satisfaction. Jamal clapped his hands. “Bas, ho gaya! Let’s go back.”

At once the boys scrambled to climb again — Nadeem, Razi and Shakeel rushing to the roof of the cabin, while the rest squeezed into the back, sitting on top of the wood itself, legs dangling. The truck roared to life, ready to carry the load — and the laughter — back to the marriage house.

Each time, it reminded him of Anwar’s warnings, and Nadeem’s heart confirmed what he had been told.

Yet, Jamal was the kind of boy who could not be unnoticed. Wherever he went, people turned towards him. He had a dashing smile on his face, spoke with confidence, and carried himself as if nothing in the world could shake him. He cracked jokes, made everyone laugh, and spread an energy that pulled people in. Everything about him looked good on the surface — the way he dressed, the way he talked, even the way he walked with that fearless air.

And this was the strange thing: Nadeem too was attracted towards Jamal. There was something in his liveliness, his easy confidence, that caught Nadeem’s attention again and again. He kept a distance, building a wall of silence, afraid to let himself be fooled by the charm that seemed to win everyone else.

The truck rolled back and stopped in front of Nadeem’s house on the main highway. There was good space between the road and the house, enough for chairs and tables to be placed for the gathering. This would be the store for all the wood that would burn for hours under the big chulahs when the feast was prepared.

Faheem’s friends enjoyed the work like it was a game. They laughed loudly, cracked jokes while carrying the bundles, and teased each other all along the staircase. Men were rarely allowed inside the house while the ladies were there. Only close family members could go in. It was a proper marriage-house atmosphere: chairs filled with people, cups of tea moving hand to hand, laughter mixing with the sound of passing trucks on the highway, and the house glowing with the busyness of preparation.

Chapter Eleven

The Jode Evening & the Girl in the Black Burqa

Rukhsana · October 2001 · Sambhal

By evening, the house was glowing with activity. Inside, all the dahez was displayed in full order — rows of utensils shining, furniture arranged neatly, clothes hanging on ropes with posters of film stars pasted behind them. Women from the town started arriving in groups, dressed in their best, faces covered with veils and burqas. “Nadeem, it’s you?” Jamal’s voice was flat but softer than usual.

Nadeem hesitated, his steps slowing. He had always tried to keep some distance from Jamal, but something in Jamal’s tone that night was different. Carrying the bundle of wood, he came closer.

“I’ll sit after I drop this to Ammi,” Nadeem said.

“I’m waiting,” Jamal replied simply, his eyes lowering again.

“You never talk much to me,” Jamal said, his voice flat but carrying a faint hurt.

Nadeem gave a weak smile. “No, it’s not like that. You misunderstood.”

Jamal studied his face for a moment, then looked away. “You don’t like me, do you?”

“No, no… not like that,” Nadeem replied quickly, still avoiding his eyes. He felt uncomfortable, smiling faintly as though covering up.

“I’ve seen you many times in Delhi,” Jamal continued. “Standing on your balcony, sitting on the water tank, reading… or lost in thoughts. I wanted to talk, but you never gave me a chance.”

“I was waiting for you,” Jamal said with a half-smile.

“Me? Why?” Nadeem asked, puzzled.

“Just kidding,” Jamal said, leaning back on his chair. “Actually, I was feeling a headache.”

For a while, silence settled between them. The sound of laughter, the clatter of plates, and the humming of the generator rose from below. A faint smell of biryani mixed with the smoke reached them. Above, the sky was scattered with stars, dim behind the haze.

“You talk very little, Nadeem,” Jamal broke the silence again.

“What should I say?” Nadeem shrugged.

“You should talk more… make friends, laugh. I see you always quiet, or only with the same people. You don’t like to mix?”

“I do have friends,” Nadeem replied. “Just not too many.”

Jamal smiled faintly, dimples forming on his cheeks. “I talk to everyone… but sometimes it feels like no one really talks to me.” His voice lowered, heavy, almost tired.

Nadeem looked at him curiously. For the first time, he saw Jamal not as the cheerful, confident boy who drew everyone’s attention, but as someone carrying something unsaid. His face in the dim light looked different, his eyes holding a weight that his smile couldn’t hide.

They kept talking like this for a long time, Jamal speaking more, Nadeem mostly listening. The noise from downstairs slowly faded as the guests began to leave, leaving the house quieter. The girls who had been peeping from the other rooftop also disappeared. The night grew colder, and the two of them eventually lay down on the cot kept near the stack of wood, still talking under the open sky until sleep silently took them both.

Chapter Fourteen

The Feast, the Baraat and the Nikah

The Wedding Day · October 2001 · Sambhal

The marriage feast started early, around eleven in the morning. In Sambhal, almost the whole town was usually invited, so the preparations had to begin hours before. Nothing was readymade in those days. Everything was done by hand—crushing the spices on heavy sil-battas, cleaning and sifting sacks of flour for the rotis, washing tubs of meat, and arranging the utensils.

The sitting arrangements were also simple. A large empty plot just next to Nadeem’s house, with a high iron gate opening onto the road, was chosen for the men’s feast. Hired tants were stretched overhead, only enough to cover the space and keep the food safe from dust, crows, and the occasional eagle circling above. Inside, long rows of wooden tables and chairs were lined neatly, waiting for the endless stream of guests.

The food was traditional—nothing fancy, yet rich and filling. Korma, or as many called it, salan. Along with it were the giant rotis, called naand, each as wide as a man’s arm-span. These were baked one by one in the tandoor, puffing up with heat, and carried hot to the serving boys who tore them into pieces according to need.

When the first group of guests sat down, the rush began. Voices calling “Naand!” and “Salan!” rose from every table. Boys serving at each line kept running back and forth—rushing to the tandoor for fresh rotis, carrying big steel dishes of steaming korma from the degs, ladling it into the small mud bowls placed in front of each guest. These bowls, handmade and slightly rough at the edges, gave a special taste to the food. Eating hot non-veg korma with naand in those clay bowls was something unmatched.

The feast had its own rhythm. People kept coming in groups, sitting, eating, and leaving, making space for the next batch. The serving boys had no time to rest, their voices hoarse from shouting, their clothes sprinkled with flour and curry stains. And when one group of boys grew tired, others who had already eaten would jump up to take their place, keeping the cycle running.

The air was filled with smoke from the wood fires, the smell of roasted meat and fried masala, the chatter of people meeting each other, and the constant clinking of steel plates and bowls. It wasn’t fancy, but it was grand in its own way—simple, tiring, and full of life.

The barat had come. All the guests entered the plot through the front area of Nadeem’s house, since the uneven ground and waterlogging made direct entry from the road impossible.

Jamal knew the groom well. In Sambhal, more than ninety percent of people were connected to the truck business in one way or another—some owned trucks, some drove them, some worked as mechanics, and others were transporters. Jamal’s father was a trader in the mandi, buying grains from farmers and transporting them to Delhi. Because of this, Jamal knew almost every truck driver, owner, and mechanic in town.

He went straight to the groom, teasing him and talking cheerfully. The groom, called dulha in the local tongue, became almost his responsibility. Jamal made him sit comfortably on a takhat—a wooden bedstead with a mattress and a fresh bedsheet laid especially for the groom. Standing beside him like a younger brother, Jamal called out in a loud voice,“Nadeem! Bring sharbat for Bhaijaan!”

Nadeem, who was busy serving food, turned at his voice. He pushed his way through the crowded rows of people who had come with the barat, stepped out of the iron gate, and returned after a while carrying a big glass jug filled to the brim with milk sharbat.

As soon as Nadeem came closer, Jamal snatched the jug from his hand with a playful authority.“Bring glasses too,” he said quickly, before Nadeem could even set the jug down.

Nadeem went to the corner of the plot where a man stood washing endless piles of dishes returning from the tables. He made a stack of glasses and brought them back carefully in both hands.

“Bhaijaan,” Jamal announced with a grin, “you know Nadeem. He is your sala—your brother-in-law.”

On this sudden introduction, Nadeem felt a little nervous. He was never comfortable speaking much with new people, and his quietness became heavier under so many watching eyes. The groom too seemed shy. He kept a hanky near his mouth, wiping his forehead now and then. He was dressed in a formal suit, with a black-rose patterned Islamic cap that looked a little too warm for the afternoon. Lifting his eyes only for a moment, he smiled faintly at Nadeem, then lowered his gaze again.

Jamal, however, kept up a stream of chitchat and playful remarks, making the groom laugh despite his shyness. Other friends of the groom joined in, asking him to eat more, joking loudly that today was his last day of freedom, teasing him whenever he pushed his plate away. The dulha, already the center of attention, looked even more embarrassed. He only picked at his food, eating a little, smiling quietly, while the people around him laughed at every new joke.

Soon trays of sweets and bottles of cold drinks arrived. Jamal himself served the dulha, raising the glass toward him with exaggerated respect, as though serving a king. Everyone clapped and whistled, enjoying the moment, while the groom lowered his eyes again, shaking his head with a half smile.

As the noise of laughter and teasing filled the plot, a voice of salaam rose from the entrance. The molana had arrived for the nikah. Instantly the mood changed. The friends moved aside to clear the space around the takhat. The groom sat straighter, still quiet, still shy, but now the eyes of everyone were on him for a different reason.

The molana, dressed in a white kurta-pajama with a shawl on his shoulder, sat on a chair brought near the groom. The loud chatter softened into murmurs, only the faint sound of the generator and the clinking of dishes remained in the background. Jamal, who had been joking just moments ago, now stepped back respectfully, folding his hands in front of him, his eyes lowered.

The nikah began. The words of the khutbah echoed in the air, slow and firm. People sitting on chairs leaned forward, listening carefully, while the younger boys peeped from behind shoulders. The groom responded softly to the molana’s questions, his voice low but steady. With each reply, a small cheer rose among the boys standing near the takhat.

Nadeem stood at the side, watching everything quietly. The white bedsheet, the groom’s bowed head, Jamal’s shining face standing close by, the molana’s voice, the smell of food still floating in the air—all of it carved deep into his memory.

The molana opened his kitab, cleared his throat, and began in a slow, steady voice. The chatter of the boys fell silent, and only the generator’s drone and the occasional clinking of dishes could be heard.

The groom sat stiffly on the takhat, eyes lowered, his hanky still in his hand. Jamal stood to his side like a younger brother, smiling lightly but with respect.

Then came the most awaited moment. The molana turned towards the dulha and asked firmly,

“Nikah qubool hai?”

The groom, with his head still bowed, replied softly, “Qubool hai.”

A second time the molana repeated, “Nikah qubool hai?”

Again the groom answered, “Qubool hai.”

And for the third time, louder so everyone could hear, the molana asked, “Nikah qubool hai?”

The groom, this time raising his voice a little, answered clearly, “Qubool hai.”

Sangam Park

After reaching Sangam Park, the place where Nadeem lived with his family in Delhi, he turned right toward his building. Chapter Twenty-Two

The First Letter

An Older Wound · 2001 · Delhi

After Jamal left that night, around ten, Nadeem lay awake for a long time. The things Jamal had said kept replaying in his head. How easily he talked about girls, love, and heartbreak — as if such things were a part of life, nothing to hide.

8th Standard · Delhi

“Even silence can teach more than scolding ever could.”

Chapter Twenty-Three

Night Reflections

“Age doesn’t matter in friendship. Hearts don’t keep count.”

“Ya Allah, why do hearts remember what minds try to forget?”

“Maybe,” he thought, “some friendships begin where old loves end — quietly, without asking for permission.”

Chapter Twenty-Four

The Evening Before Leaving

The sky was half-orange, half-blue, when Nadeem came up to the roof. The air was still — that quiet time between the call of the Maghrib and the slow hum of night. Across the narrow street, Naina was standing at the doorway of her ground-floor flat, leaning against the wall beside the park gate. Her dupatta slipped lightly from her head, hair catching the faint glow of the nearby shop’s bulb. She was pretending to look around, but her eyes found Jamal as soon as he turned into the lane.

Jamal noticed too.

He didn’t stop, didn’t wave — just lifted his chin slightly, a small gesture only she could understand. She smiled — that nervous, shy smile that flickers and disappears — and began walking ahead, slowly, in front of Jamal.

It was quiet — the sound of footsteps, the faint laughter of a child playing somewhere far. When Naina reached near her building’s corner, she stopped, looked left and right to make sure no one was watching.

Jamal came up close behind her. He said something soft — Nadeem couldn’t hear the words — and she turned, smiling in that quiet, uncertain way. He held her hand gently, and she didn’t pull back. Instead, she smiled wider, and Jamal leaned forward — kissed the back of her hand. She looked down, then up at him again — a quick moment of stillness — and he kissed her lips.

The world stayed still for those few seconds — no voice, no footsteps, only the night air between them.

Then Naina pulled away quickly, checked the street again, and walked back to the same spot where she’d been standing earlier — leaned against the wall like nothing happened — before quietly entering her house.

Jamal stood for a moment, smiling to himself, then looked up — and his eyes met Nadeem’s, who was still on the roof, frozen in place. Jamal smiled again, that mischievous, knowing smile, and started climbing the stairs to Nadeem’s floor.

Nadeem looked at him, a mix of amusement and disbelief. “I saw that,” he said quietly.

Jamal grinned. “Of course you did. I knew you were watching.”

“You didn’t even care if someone else saw?” Nadeem asked.

Jamal shrugged lightly. “She smiled, and I… smiled back.”

“You kissed her,” Nadeem said, almost scolding, but with a shy laugh.

“That too,” Jamal said, grinning. “When life gives you a smile, don’t waste it.”

They both laughed, the night air carrying their voices softly across the open rooftops.

“I’m leaving for Sambhal tomorrow morning,” Jamal said after a moment.

“So soon?” Nadeem asked.

“Hmm. Some work there, some faces to see,” Jamal said, brushing his hair back with his fingers.

Nadeem smiled, looking down. “You’re not afraid of anyone, are you?”

Jamal’s voice softened, his smile fading into something deeper.

“Afraid? Maybe. But I’ve learned — fear wastes the beauty of a moment.”

“Some people wait for the right time, Nadeem. Others make the moment right.”

Then he disappeared down the stairs, his footsteps fading.

Nadeem sat there for a long time, staring at the dark lane where Naina had stood. He didn’t judge what he saw — only thought about how differently hearts worked: some wrote letters and waited for courage; others kissed the moment before it could escape.

Chapter Twenty-Five

A Year of Growing Up, and the Call

After that last trip from Sambhal, Nadeem didn’t go there again for almost a year. His 10th standard final exams were near, and his parents wanted him to stay in Delhi and focus. Chapter Twenty-Six

The Days Between School and Home

Pradeep & Delhi · 2001–2005 · Delhi

Delhi had its own rhythm, and Nadeem knew it by heart.

The school never took the whole day. It started in the afternoon, because the morning shift belonged to the girls, and by the time the boys went in and the last teacher disappeared into the staff room, most of the day was already behind them. What remained — those long hours of nothing — belonged to the streets, to the colony, to whoever was willing to walk with him.

Pradeep Singh was always willing.

They had known each other since childhood. Same school, same neighbourhood, same understanding that no one needed a reason to knock on the other's door. Whenever Nadeem came down from the flat and found Pradeep outside, or Pradeep saw Nadeem crossing the road, they would fall into step together as if they had already agreed on a plan.

They never had a plan.

They had no vehicle either. No bicycle, no scooter, nothing. Their feet carried them wherever the afternoon decided to go. Sangam Park was right next to Ashok Vihar — once they crossed the park, Ashok Vihar began, and between the two areas ran a railway line. That railway line became part of their world.

One of the things they both loved was fish.

Not eating fish. Keeping fish. Small, bright, beautiful fish in glass tanks at home.

In Ashok Vihar there were several roadside fish shops. They were nothing like proper aquarium stores. The shopkeepers had built them by hand — wooden frames, rough grass-like sheets nailed to the sides, simple shelves stacked one above the other. Small aquariums sat on those shelves, some barely the size of a shoebox, and inside them tiny fish moved through the water in circles that had no beginning and no end.

Behind some of the shops there were small nurseries where plants were kept — little green things in plastic pots, the kind that could survive on almost no attention. Sometimes Nadeem and Pradeep would look at those too. If they liked one, they bought it for the pots at home.

But it was always the fish that brought them there.

Nadeem had a weakness for a particular orange-golden fish. Bright, clean, almost glowing in the water. Whenever he saw one, his eyes stayed on it a little longer than the others.

There was another fish he liked even more. It was thin, almost leaf-shaped, black in colour, with darker black markings running across its body. Something about its shape fascinated him. Years later, when he saw Finding Nemo for the first time, something about that fish reminded him of this one — and the memory came back as clearly as if he was still standing outside that roadside shop in Ashok Vihar, twelve years old, with Pradeep beside him.

Sometimes they bought fish.

Most times they did not.

They would simply stand there, look at the aquariums, watch the fish moving, and then start walking again.

Pradeep knew everything about Nadeem. He was that kind of friend — the one who listened without needing to be told why the conversation mattered. Whenever Nadeem returned from Sambhal, the first thing he did after reaching Delhi was sit with Pradeep and talk. He told him about Sambhal, about the lanes and the shops and the people, about Faheem Bhai, about the tea stall, and — more than anything else — about Jamal.

Nadeem talked about Jamal so much that Pradeep had already built a picture of him in his head. He knew Jamal's habits, his way of talking, the things he had said to Nadeem on the roof, on the walks, at the tea stall. Jamal had become a character in Pradeep's imagination long before Pradeep had ever seen him properly.

Then one afternoon, while Nadeem and Pradeep were walking near the school, Jamal appeared.

Just like that. Without warning.

Nadeem saw him first.

His face changed immediately. Something lit up behind his eyes, the kind of shift that is impossible to hide from someone who knows you well.

Pradeep noticed.

He looked at Nadeem, then at the man Nadeem was looking at, then back at Nadeem.

"Who is that?" Pradeep asked. "Why are you looking like that?"

"That's Jamal," Nadeem said.

He said it quietly, the way one says a name that has been spoken a hundred times in conversation but never once in person.

They kept walking, but after some time Pradeep complained. "Yaar, you should have told me. If I knew he was there, I would have gone and talked to him too."

That was Pradeep.

He was not a particularly good student. In fact, most of the boys in their school were not interested in studying at all. It was a government school with poor facilities — sometimes there were no fans, sometimes there was no drinking water, and the teachers often stayed in the staff room for hours without entering a single classroom. After the morning assembly the boys would scatter into their rooms, but the day did not always turn into a day of studying.

They played. They talked. They wandered from one classroom to another. They made noise and they enjoyed themselves. There was a freedom in that disorder that they did not understand at the time.

Nadeem was one of the few boys the teachers called intelligent. Even that intelligence was a simple thing — he could read Hindi properly, and he could read English better than most of the boys around him. Whenever a teacher actually came into the classroom and asked someone to stand up and read from a book, Nadeem was usually the one called.

That was enough to make him stand out in a school where standing out required very little.

The school ran in two shifts. Girls in the morning, boys in the afternoon.

So there was a strange daily rhythm at the school gate.

Every afternoon, just before the boys went in, the girls were coming out. The boys would stand outside and wait — not because they needed to, but because they wanted to see. There was teasing, gestures, jokes shouted from a distance. Some girls blushed and walked away quickly. Some looked angrily at the boys and continued without responding. Some girls simply ignored everything, as if the boys did not exist at all.

And sometimes the girls hit back.

When the boys entered the classrooms, they would find desks turned upside down. A line might be written on the blackboard. A message might have been left behind on a desk, addressed to no one and meant for someone.

Sometimes the boys would write back: "Please don't turn the desks upside down."

And the next morning, the girls would leave their own reply.

There was a corridor in the school that was enclosed on all four sides. That corridor became a kind of secret wall.

There were no mobile phones. No WhatsApp. No way for boys and girls from different shifts to talk to each other.

So they used the walls.

Names were written in shortened forms. Initials were used. Little codes were created so that a particular girl or boy would understand who the message was for, while everyone else would simply see marks on a wall.

Sometimes a message did not even need a name.

The right person just knew.

Nadeem himself had no girlfriend. His interest in girls was the innocent, half-formed curiosity of that age — noticing someone, thinking about her for a few days, sometimes writing a letter that was never properly delivered. There was never a relationship, never a plan to meet someone, never anything beyond glances and imagination and the kind of wondering that fills the space between fifteen and eighteen.

Pradeep's house was directly opposite the school gate. Only a road separated the two.

His mother was strict. Very strict.

She would sit outside the house on four or five chairs arranged near the entrance, watching the road, watching the school gate, watching Pradeep — and she did not like him going anywhere.

Nadeem developed his own system.

He would come near the house, carefully check whether Pradeep's mother was looking or not, and then signal to Pradeep from a safe distance. A wave, a gesture, a quick movement of the head. Pradeep would see it and know — it was time to come out.

Sometimes it worked.

Sometimes it did not.

If she saw Nadeem signalling, she would call him over.

"Nadeem, idhar aao."

Nadeem's stomach would drop.

She would make him sit and ask why he was always taking Pradeep out of the house. "Kyun har waqt ghumte rehte ho? Ghar pe baitho. Yaheen khelo. Kahan jaana hai tumhe? Baitho yaheen, meri aankhon ke saamne."

"Okay, Aunty."

That was the only thing Nadeem could ever manage to say.

She frightened him. But she was also kind to him. Whenever he came to their house, she gave him something to eat — fruit, snacks, whatever was there. Pradeep's father liked him too, and so did Pradeep's two brothers. The whole family liked Nadeem.

Her only condition was simple — if Pradeep was going to spend time with anyone, he should do it right there, in front of the house, where she could see them.

The funny thing was that Pradeep and Nadeem did not even play much.

They talked.

They had endless things to talk about. They made fun of the people passing by on the road. They would look at someone — the way he was walking, the expression on his face, the strange angle of his cap — and whisper to each other and laugh. At that age, the whole world was material for comedy.

"Dekh dekh, usko dekh."

"Usse bhi dekh."

Then both would laugh like they had heard the greatest joke in the world.

And when Pradeep's mother was not looking, they escaped.

Their walks often took them towards the railway line. The track ran between Sangam Park and Ashok Vihar. There were grey and brown stones scattered along the sides, and sometimes the two boys would sit there, picking up stones and looking at their shapes, as if the stones had some meaning that only bored, happy boys could understand.

Like many foolish boys of that age, they also did stupid things.

They would place a coin on the railway track and wait.

They wanted to see what the train would do to it.

When the train came, they would stand nearby and wave at the passengers inside. The people would disappear from their view almost instantly — the windows flashing past so fast that no face had time to register — but for those few seconds, Nadeem and Pradeep would wave their hands wildly and laugh, as if they had made some kind of connection with a hundred strangers they would never see again.

They also loved watching airplanes.

Whenever one appeared in the sky — it did not matter where Nadeem was, on the road, at school, near a window at home — he would stop and look up. There was something about the size, the sound, the way it moved so slowly across the sky even though he knew it was going impossibly fast. Sometimes he and Pradeep would imitate the sound of the airplane themselves, holding their arms out, making the noise with their mouths.

It was such a small, stupid thing. But it made him happy.

The railway line, however, eventually taught them something they never forgot.

One afternoon they were crossing the track. They were talking, laughing, not paying attention — the way they always crossed, the way careless boys always cross.

Then a train horn exploded beside them.

It was so loud and so close that for a moment everything in Nadeem's body stopped responding. His legs would not move. His hands reached out blindly. The train was right there — inches away, enormous, impossibly fast.

Both boys screamed.

They grabbed each other instinctively, their hands locking onto each other's arms, their feet frozen between the two tracks. They could not go forward. They could not safely go back. And the train came past them at a speed that turned the air into a wall.

The wind slammed against their faces.

The sound was so enormous that it filled everything — it was inside their ears, inside their chests, inside the bones of their legs.

For those few moments, the train seemed to have no end.

Then it was gone.

Pradeep and Nadeem were still standing there, holding onto each other, their fingers still locked into each other's shirts.

Neither of them spoke.

Their hearts were pounding.

They slowly walked away from the track and stood at a distance, breathing hard, staring at the empty rails where the train had just been. For a long time they said nothing. Then Pradeep looked at Nadeem. And Nadeem looked at Pradeep.

And they started laughing.

It was that strange laughter that comes after a great fear — the body not knowing whether to cry or to laugh, so it does both at once.

But after that day, something changed.

Whenever they crossed the railway line, they stopped talking. No jokes. No stories. No looking at stones. They looked left. Then right. Then left again. Only when both of them were sure that no train was coming from either direction did they step onto the track.

That one moment had been enough to teach two careless boys a lesson that no teacher ever could have.

There were other places they liked too.

Their walks sometimes took them past the private schools in Ashok Vihar. The buildings looked different from their own school. The grounds were cleaner. The swings looked better. The uniforms were neat — not like the crumpled, faded ones the boys wore at their government school.

Sometimes, especially in the morning when the girls' shift was running, they would pass by and see the students coming out in their uniforms.

Pradeep would look at them carefully. He was especially interested in the clothes — the blazers, the ties, the way certain fabrics looked on certain people. He wanted to become a fashion designer. That was his dream, spoken without embarrassment, even though nobody around him understood what fashion designing even meant.

Nadeem would look at those schools and feel something else. Not jealousy exactly, but a quiet wondering.

What if he studied in a school like that?

What if he wore a uniform like those?

He never had an answer. He simply kept walking.

Near their school there was a Delhi Public Library.

Not many people went there. Inside, there were shelves of Hindi and English books, arranged neatly, untouched by most visitors. The library was usually quiet. Most of the time there was only one librarian.

She was a strict woman. She wore glasses, but one side of the glasses was broken, and she wore the remaining side in a way that made her look even more serious to the boys than she probably was.

Whenever Nadeem and Pradeep entered, she immediately started watching them. She knew they were not the kind of boys who came to sit quietly and read for hours. She was right.

Nadeem usually picked up storybooks. Sometimes he read. Sometimes he just looked through the pictures.

Pradeep had another purpose. He would open books with photographs of clothes, models, jackets, coats — anything related to fashion. He could spend a long time on a single page, studying the cut of a sleeve, the shape of a collar, the way a coat fell on a model's shoulders.

One day, while the librarian's attention was elsewhere, Pradeep found a page that stopped him.

A model wearing jeans and a smart-looking coat. Something about the outfit caught his eye the way the orange-golden fish had once caught Nadeem's.

Pradeep quietly tore the page out.

Nadeem saw him do it.

His stomach tightened. "Mat kar," he kept whispering. "Chhod de, rakh de wapas."

But Pradeep had already decided. He folded the page carefully, slipped it into his pocket, closed the book, returned it to the desk, and the two boys left the library as if nothing had happened.

For the next two or three days, Nadeem could not stop worrying.

"Police ko bolegi," he told Pradeep. "Pakde jaayenge."

Pradeep was worried too. But he did not give the page back.

The librarian eventually warned them. She told them that these were government books and nobody was allowed to tear their pages. She did not say it kindly.

After that, whenever Nadeem and Pradeep went back to the library, she watched them like a hawk. Sometimes she made them sit right next to her. No more wandering among the shelves. No more freedom.

But even that did not stop them from going back.

There were too many books.

At first, Nadeem mostly read Hindi books. Hindi was comfortable. Hindi was home.

English was different. English books looked interesting — thick spines, clean covers, words printed in a font that looked more serious than Hindi. But when he opened one and tried to read, he understood almost nothing.

Then he saw a book called The Book Thief.

The cover caught him immediately. There was a small girl on it, and somewhere behind her, an image that looked like the Angel of Death. He did not fully understand what the picture was showing, but something about it pulled him.

He picked it up.

He tried to read.

He read a few lines.

He stopped.

He understood almost nothing.

But that book would not leave his head. He came back to it the next time he visited the library, and the time after that. He kept picking it up, reading a few more lines, failing to understand, and putting it down again.

Eventually, he issued it.

He brought it home.

He took a dictionary.

And he began.

He would read one passage, then stop at almost every word he did not know. He would open the dictionary, find the meaning, and write the Hindi translation in pencil above the English word. Then he would go back to the beginning of the passage and read it again.

The next day, he would do the same.

One or two passages at a time.

Every unfamiliar word.

Every meaning.

Every sentence.

Slowly, the pages began filling with his pencil marks. The book became less like a novel and more like a personal English textbook — every page covered in Nadeem's handwriting, every difficult word carrying a small Hindi translation above it, every passage read and reread until the words started making sense.

He did not finish quickly.

It took him almost three years.

But he finished it.

And somewhere during those three years, something changed inside him that was bigger than any English lesson. Words that had once looked like walls began to open. He started remembering meanings without needing the dictionary. His reading became faster. His confidence with English grew.

No teacher had sat beside him. No one had enrolled him in a course or paid for tuitions. He had simply wanted to understand one book badly enough to keep trying, and somewhere in that stubbornness, he had taught himself English.

Sometimes curiosity itself could become a teacher.

Sometimes a dictionary could become a classroom.

Sometimes the book you could barely read was the very book that taught you how to read.

For Pradeep, the world was still full of clothes, fabrics, designs, and details to notice.

For Nadeem, it was slowly becoming full of words.

And between the two of them, there was always another road to walk, another fish to look at, another train to wave at, another airplane to watch, another story to bring back from Sambhal, and another reason to leave the house and simply be together.

They thought they were only wasting time.

Years later, Nadeem understood that they had not been wasting it at all.

They were living it.

The Night We Walked Together

Where It Began · Winter 2002 · Sambhal

Years have passed, but every time the winter fog rolls over the narrow lanes of Sambhal, Nadeem finds himself standing on that dark road again.

He remembers the dim streetlights beaming weakly through the mist, the sound of laughter echoing ahead, and the sixteen-year-old girl walking beside him, completely lost in her own carefree chatter.

She never knew. She never realized that when her hand brushed his in the freezing night air, it wasn't just a fleeting second. For him, it was a spark that lit an eternal flame. In that single touch, two lives were woven together across a decade of trials, whispered hopes, and enduring devotion.

Jamal taught him that love can break you in ways the world will never see. But walking beside Shalini taught him that true love also heals, endures, and outlasts every storm.

They walked together that night into the fog, uncertain of what lay ahead. And even now, with all the roads behind him, he knows he would walk that path with her again, through every lifetime.
